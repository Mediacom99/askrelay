## Recommendation: **KEEP_APACHE_PURE_OSS**

**Keep Apache-2.0 + free hard-quota hosted demo: it is the option-MAXIMIZING posture, not a constraint — D-13's recorded premise ("DCO makes the license effectively immutable") is inverted and must be corrected in the log; the real pre-launch locks are D-08's social framing and Kosmoy employer-IP under Italian art. 12-bis, not licensing.**

## Rationale

Three verified legs converge, and all four briefs independently concluded "keep D-13." (1) NOTHING TO MONETIZE YET: the exact category (cross-person, approval-gated AI-session messaging) has zero documented willingness to pay — BAND ($17M seed, only direct competitor) has no published pricing or named customers; HumanLayer abandoned its approval API for a per-seat IDE, signaling approval-gating is a feature, not a product. At askrelay's modal outcome (dozens of stars, employer as only heavy user, free→paid conversion well under 1%) a paid tier earns ~€0–200/month while adding billing/support/uptime ops; even the winners needed years (Plausible: 11 months to $400 MRR in a high-demand category; Healthchecks: 6.5 years to sole income; Zulip, the closest domain comparable: a decade of founder subsidy then a 2026 nonprofit pivot; Papercups shut its cloud despite YC money). Switching posture now changes nothing about near-term revenue while spending the project's two real assets: the flagship-OSS goal and the trust story of a consent/security product. (2) THE TRADE-OFF IS LEGALLY INVERTED: Apache-2.0 §2 grants perpetual, irrevocable sublicense and derivative-work rights, so even after external DCO contributions the maintainer can move FUTURE versions to AGPL/BSL/open-core/proprietary without contributor consent (Synapse, DCO-only, executed Apache→AGPL Dec 2023; MinIO 2021) — the open-core decision is deferrable at near-zero legal cost, and Sidekiq/Caddy-style maintainer-authored closed add-ons or hosted-ops code never require any relicense at all. The AGPL-relay fallback is the ONLY direction that hard-locks (AGPL-inbound + DCO forecloses permissive return and proprietary exceptions without unanimous consent — why Element added a CLA at its switch). It should be formally retired, not exercised: AGPL carries documented enterprise adoption friction (Google's hard ban, CNCF Apache default) fatal to a category-creation tool that must spread frictionlessly inside companies, and it would not stop the realistic clone vector anyway (clean-room reimplementation of an open protocol — DocumentDB, Vaultwarden). The AGPL advocate's best point — Plausible thrives on AGPL and was cloned at small scale — buys insurance on a house that will likely never be built, at the cost of the one thing askrelay must have: frictionless in-company spread. (3) TIMING EVIDENCE FAVORS HONEST-FREE-NOW WITH STATED PAID INTENT: no verified case shows day-one commercial terms slowing adoption (Plausible charged before open-sourcing; n8n restricted from first release, 197k stars), and backlash attaches only to dishonest labeling and retroactive enclosure (n8n threads, MinIO 2021, Caddy 2017, Cal.com April 2026). The open-core advocate's genuinely sound points — ntfy's 25k-users/20-payers expectation trap and the value of pre-announcing that the hosted tier MAY become paid — are absorbed into a D-08 framing revision without publishing pricing (which would price an undemonstrated product and sharpen the unresolved art. 2105 duty-of-loyalty question before the lawyer consult). Proprietary: the maintainer's rejection stands unchallenged — the evidence affirms it (zero WTP to capture, certain trust cost for a consent/security product); no brief contradicts the reasoning. First-dollar path at this scale is Kosmoy sponsorship and GitHub Sponsors, never hosting invoices.

## What locks, when (the verified mechanics)

VERIFIED AGAINST APACHE-2.0 §2/§4 TEXT AND FSF/ASF COMPATIBILITY PAGES — the direction of the DCO lock is the OPPOSITE of D-13's recorded premise. (1) AT FIRST PUBLICATION (before any contribution): exclusivity locks irrevocably. Every line shipped under Apache-2.0 stays Apache-licensed, forkable, and commercially hostable by anyone — including a cloud vendor or Kosmoy — forever; this cements line-by-line as code ships and merges. Any later restrictive pivot competes against its own free fork (MinIO/OpenSearch/OpenTofu/Valkey pattern). (2) AT FIRST EXTERNAL DCO CONTRIBUTION (Apache-inbound): nothing NEW locks in the restrictive direction. Apache-2.0 §2 grants every licensee, including the maintainer, a perpetual irrevocable right to sublicense and to distribute derivative works under different terms (§4 requires only notice retention), so FUTURE versions can lawfully move to AGPL, BSL, open-core, or fully proprietary without contributor consent — executed precedent: Synapse (verified DCO-only, Apache→AGPL Dec 2023) and MinIO (2021), no consent campaign, no litigation. Caveat for the log: no court has ever ruled on unilateral Apache→AGPL forward relicensing; this rests on settled practice plus license text. (3) THE ONE GENUINE LICENSING DEADLINE: the documented AGPL-relay fallback hard-locks at first external contribution — once AGPL-inbound code contains external DCO'd contributions, returning to permissive or selling proprietary exceptions requires unanimous contributor consent or rewrite (Element added a CLA at its switch moment for exactly this reason). It must be exercised before meaningful external contributions or never; recommendation: retire it now. (4) NEVER LOCKS under Apache+DCO: paid hosted tier, proprietary hosted-ops/billing code kept out of the OSS repo, Sidekiq/Caddy-style separately-authored closed add-ons, open-core with new closed components beside the Apache core. (5) THE CLOCKS THAT ACTUALLY RUN EARLY ARE NOT LEGAL: (a) social — hosted-service expectations lock at D-08's public launch (ntfy: 25k daily users, 20 payers, quota retreat 17k→250 msgs/day, paid launch killed donations), and relicensing/backlash cost scales with community size (Plausible at ~zero users paid nothing; HashiCorp spawned OpenTofu); (b) employer-IP — Italian art. 12-bis exposure and Kosmoy colleagues' hollow DCO sign-offs must be resolved before launch and before their first patches. Scale caveat: all comparators had 10k+ stars; there is no data at dozens-of-stars scale in either direction.

## Entity & sponsor note

The real pre-launch lock risk is employer-IP, not licensing. Italian art. 12-bis L.633/1941 assigns the employer software created "in the performance of duties or on instructions"; Kosmoy as first user, dogfooding host, and prospective founding sponsor puts askrelay squarely in that grey zone, and Bologna 96/2020 shows courts read the prong broadly. Highest-value single action, before launch and before any Kosmoy colleague's first patch: a written IP acknowledgment letter from Kosmoy naming askrelay, in FSF-disclaimer/BEIPA-2.0 style (employee owns; employer receives a non-exclusive use license). TAKE TO AN ITALIAN LAWYER, as one consult: (1) the exact wording of the employment contract's IP clause — a "patto contrario" can cut either way; (2) whether work done at Kosmoy's request or on Kosmoy hours triggers the 12-bis "instructions" prong; (3) whether a future paid hosted service by a Kosmoy employee whose employer is the first user conflicts with the art. 2105 duty of loyalty — this is a hard prerequisite to ever publishing a price; (4) the form of lightweight employer disclaimers for Kosmoy colleagues who contribute, since 12-bis makes their patches Kosmoy's by default and their DCO sign-offs may otherwise be hollow. OWNERSHIP DECISIONS THAT CANNOT WAIT (filings can): record now that the trademark, GitHub org, and domains are owned by Edoardo personally, never by Kosmoy — Redis is the documented worst case of a founding sponsor absorbing a maintainer's project name/IP; curl (personal ownership, employer-as-sponsor, no entity) is the benchmark; sponsor CREDIT is safe, sponsor ownership of mark/org/domain is not. This reinforces D-14 (neutral org later, Kosmoy credited as founding sponsor) and leaves D-17's traction-triggered FILING timeline untouched. askrelay.dev is already personally owned — keep it that way. Form no entity now; incorporate (SRL or ditta individuale) only when revenue is imminent; if paid add-ons ever ship, follow the Sidekiq pattern (separate repo, maintainer-only authorship, entity at commercialization).

## Advocate panel

### apache-pure

The case for keeping Apache-2.0 + free hosted demo rests on three independently verified legs, and each was strengthened — not weakened — by adversarial verification.

LEG 1: There is no revenue to protect, so a restrictive posture buys nothing. The market verdict is GO_WITH_CHANGES with zero expressed demand and a modal outcome of dozens of stars with the employer as only heavy user. At that scale, the hosted-economics brief is unambiguous: free-to-paid conversion runs well under 1%, so a paid tier at the modal outcome earns approximately EUR 0–200/month while adding billing, support, and uptime obligations. Every project with material hosting revenue had, before that revenue, either a proven already-monetized category or years of subsidized grind: Plausible (the best bootstrapped case, in a high-demand category askrelay lacks) took 11 months to $400 MRR and 3 years to $1M ARR; Healthchecks.io took 6.5 years to sole-income and sits at $14k MRR after 9 years; Zulip — the closest domain comparable — never made subscriptions carry the team in a decade and pivoted to a grant-seeking nonprofit in 2026; Papercups had YC money and stars and still shut its cloud. The WTP brief closes the loop for askrelay's specific category: cross-person, approval-gated AI-session messaging has ZERO documented willingness to pay anywhere — the only direct competitor (BAND, $17M seed) has no published pricing and no named paying customers, and HumanLayer, the closest behavioral analog, abandoned its approval API in favor of a per-seat IDE. You cannot monetize demand that no one, including funded startups, has demonstrated exists. Switching to open-core or proprietary NOW would therefore change nothing about near-term revenue while spending the two assets the project actually has: the flagship-OSS goal and the trust story of a consent/security product.

LEG 2: The apparent trade-off — 'stay open and lose commercial optionality' — is legally inverted. This is the decisive finding, confirmed against the Apache-2.0 license text (sections 2 and 4), the FSF/ASF one-way-compatibility pages, and executed precedent. Apache-2.0 grants everyone, including the maintainer, perpetual irrevocable rights to sublicense and distribute derivative works under different terms. So even after external DCO contributions, future versions can lawfully go open-core, AGPL, BSL, or fully proprietary with only notice-preservation duties, no CLA and no contributor consent needed. Synapse (verified DCO-only) and MinIO executed exactly this Apache-to-AGPL move with no consent campaign and no litigation. Meanwhile the documented fallback — AGPL for the relay — is the direction that genuinely hard-locks: AGPL-inbound with DCO forecloses returning to permissive or selling proprietary exceptions without unanimous consent, which is precisely why Element added a CLA at the moment of its switch. Apache+DCO is therefore the option-MAXIMIZING posture, and the open-core decision is safely deferrable at near-zero legal cost. The real relicensing clock is social, not legal — cost scales with community size (Plausible at ~zero users paid nothing; HashiCorp at ecosystem scale spawned OpenTofu) — and askrelay's modal outcome keeps that cost negligible for years.

LEG 3: The timing evidence says announcing paid intent early is free, while restrictive-now carries real costs. Across every verified case, charging or stating commercial intent from day one did not slow OSS adoption (Plausible charged before open-sourcing; n8n carried commercial restrictions from first release and is the most-adopted project in the sample at 197k stars). Community anger attaches to dishonest labeling and retroactive enclosure (n8n 'gaslighting' threads, Cal.com's April 2026 closed-source pivot, MinIO 2021), never to honest paid hosting. Conversely, restrictive licensing has documented enterprise adoption friction — Google's hard AGPL ban, CNCF's Apache default, blanket corporate policies — which is fatal to a category-creation tool that must spread frictionlessly inside companies. And AGPL would not even stop the realistic clone vector, since clean-room reimplementation of an open protocol bypasses any code license (DocumentDB, Vaultwarden). If anyone ever clones and undercuts, the verified moat evidence points at trademark plus operational excellence — every fork was constrained by trademarks, not licenses — which is exactly the 'compete on ops' half of this posture, and it argues for D-17's traction-triggered trademark filing with one amendment: the OWNERSHIP decision (Edoardo personally, not Kosmoy) must be documented now even though filing stays deferred, because founding-sponsor absorption of the mark (Redis) is the documented worst case for a maintainer-owned project.

### agpl-split

The verified license-mechanics evidence establishes an asymmetry that everyone reads as favoring Apache, but which actually makes the pre-launch moment the only moment Package B can ever be exercised — and my case is that an option exercisable only now, at near-zero cost, protecting the only tail outcome where money exists, should be exercised now.

First, the timing fact. Under Apache-2.0, what locks is exclusivity, and it locks at first publication, not first contribution: every shipped line stays Apache for everyone forever, so any later hosted-protection pivot (Synapse-style Apache→AGPL, which the briefs confirm is legally executable) still competes against a complete, free, Apache-licensed fork of the pre-switch relay — the MinIO/OpenSearch/OpenTofu fork-and-continue outcome, plus the Caddy-2017/Cal.com-2026 social cost that the timing-effects brief shows attaches specifically to retroactive restriction. The hosted-economics brief itself reframes the AGPL fallback as "exercise before meaningful external contributions or not at all." askrelay is pre-code. This is "before." The Apache path's celebrated optionality is real but one-directional: it preserves the option to restrict later at a price (fork + backlash) that grows with exactly the community success the project hopes for. Package B pays the price when it is zero.

Second, the adoption cost of restrictive-from-day-one is, on the verified record, approximately nothing. No case in the timing-effects brief shows early restrictive terms slowing adoption: n8n shipped commercially restricted from its first release and is the most-adopted project in the sample (197k stars); Plausible charged before it even open-sourced and its launch was warm. Community anger attaches to dishonest labeling and retroactive enclosure — never to honest terms stated at launch. AGPL is OSI-approved open source, so the flagship-OSS goal survives intact and the n8n mislabeling trap is avoided entirely. And the strongest bootstrapped hosted-OSS success in the entire evidence base — Plausible, $1M ARR — is itself AGPL, having relicensed precisely because a small, non-hyperscaler cloner appeared (a verifier-confirmed contradiction of the "only hyperscalers clone" claim). The clone threat is documented at small scale, and AGPL plus a hosted business demonstrably coexist.

Third, the trust argument — usually deployed against restrictive licensing — actually runs my way for this product. The briefs prove that under Apache+DCO the maintainer retains the unilateral right to take future versions proprietary (the exact Cal.com move, executed by Synapse and MinIO without contributor consent). That means the timing-effects brief's proposed README sentence — "no CLA means we structurally cannot pull a Cal.com" — is false under D-13 as written. It becomes true only under AGPL-inbound + DCO + no-CLA, where external contributions genuinely hard-lock everyone, including the maintainer and any future acquirer or founding sponsor, out of enclosure. For a consent/security product whose entire pitch is "the gate cannot be quietly removed," making the anti-rug-pull guarantee structural rather than a revocable promise is on-brand in a way Apache cannot match — and it also insures against the Redis scenario the entity brief flags, where a founding sponsor absorbs a maintainer's project, since Kosmoy is both first user and prospective sponsor.

Fourth, the split design confines the known costs. Clients, SDK, and daemon stay Apache-2.0, so the things that must spread frictionlessly inside companies — the code developers embed and run in their sessions — carry no AGPL friction. The AGPL surface is the relay server only: the self-hostable network service, which is exactly the artifact a future hosted competitor would take, and exactly where the WTP brief concedes that under Apache "anyone else can host it commercially and the mark is the only moat." Package B adds a second moat (competitors must publish modifications — MongoDB's own credited mechanism for a decade of protection) without touching D-17's deferred trademark timing.

Finally, the hard-lock AGPL imposes forecloses only a door the maintainer has already publicly nailed shut. AGPL+DCO prevents future proprietary exceptions and permissive return without contributor consent — but closed-source is already rejected in the stated prior, on trust grounds. The lock costs nothing relative to declared values; it merely makes the declaration binding on successors. What must change beyond D-13: D-08's hosted instance publishes its full running source (Plausible-style), which is a feature, not a burden, for a security product.

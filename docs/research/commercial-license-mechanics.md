# Commercialization research — License mechanics in practice: what DCO+Apache-2.0 actually locks, AGPL-split vs ops-moat vs fair-code, and the clone-threat evidence base

> Input to the commercialization-posture decision (D-13/D-08 review), researched
> 2026-07-19 against live sources; every evidence item independently re-checked.

## TL;DR

Apache-2.0 + DCO locks almost nothing in the direction askrelay cares about: because Apache-2.0 is one-way compatible with (A)GPLv3 and grants sublicensing, a maintainer can move FUTURE versions to AGPL, fair-code, or even proprietary without contributor consent — Synapse (DCO-only, Dec 2023) and MinIO (2021) executed exactly this Apache→AGPL move. What locks forever is only the already-shipped code (fork risk: OpenSearch, OpenTofu, Valkey) — whereas choosing AGPL-inbound now WOULD lock, since AGPL→permissive needs every contributor's consent. Clone-and-undercut is real but only for hyperscaler-scale targets, AGPL only partially deters it (MongoDB's own stated reason for SSPL) while adding documented enterprise adoption friction (Google hard ban, CNCF Apache-2.0 default); so keeping D-13 is the option-preserving choice and the open-core decision is safely deferrable.

## Assessment

The lock question resolves cleanly and mostly in the opposite direction from the worry embedded in D-13. Under inbound=outbound Apache-2.0 with DCO, contributors keep their copyright but grant everyone — including the maintainer — a perpetual, irrevocable right to sublicense and distribute derivative works under different terms, provided Apache notices are retained. Combined with FSF/ASF-confirmed one-way compatibility, that means future versions CAN move to AGPLv3 (or BSL/fair-code, or proprietary) without contributor consent; past contributions simply remain Apache-licensed inside the combined work. This is not theory: Synapse (verified DCO-only via its own 2021 contributing guide) and MinIO both executed Apache→AGPL with no contributor-consent campaign and no litigation. The genuinely irreversible facts are (1) everything already shipped stays Apache-2.0 forever — the community can always fork-and-continue (OpenSearch, OpenTofu, Valkey all prove this is a real, well-organized outcome at scale), and (2) the reverse configuration locks hard: AGPL-inbound-with-DCO forecloses returning to permissive or selling proprietary exceptions without a CLA or unanimous consent — which is exactly why Element added a CLA at the moment of its switch, not before. On clone threat: every documented clone-and-undercut case involves hyperscalers targeting infrastructure with massive installed bases; AGPL only partially deterred them (MongoDB's own SSPL rationale), Grafana solved AWS commercially, and clean-room API reimplementation (DocumentDB, Vaultwarden) bypasses any code license. Meanwhile AGPL carries documented adoption friction (Google's hard ban, CNCF's Apache default, blanket corporate policies). One caveat: no court has ever ruled on unilateral Apache→AGPL relicensing; commentary is contested even if practice is settled.

## Implications for askrelay

The maintainer's prior survives the evidence, and D-13 turns out to be the option-MAXIMIZING choice, not a constraint: Apache-2.0+DCO keeps every posture open — AGPL-relay split, fair-code, paid hosted tier, even (values aside) proprietary — for all future versions, at any time, without contributor consent. The commercialization decision is therefore deferrable, and deferral is nearly free legally. The real clock is social, not legal: relicensing cost scales with community size (Plausible at ~zero users paid nothing; HashiCorp at ecosystem scale spawned OpenTofu), but askrelay's modal outcome (dozens of stars) keeps that cost negligible for years. Do NOT preemptively adopt AGPL: it is the direction that locks (AGPL-inbound + DCO forecloses permissive return and dual-licensing), it adds enterprise adoption friction fatal to a category-creation tool that must spread frictionlessly inside companies (Google ban, blanket policies), and it would not stop the realistic clone vector anyway (clean-room reimplementation of an open protocol). Concretely: keep D-13 and D-08 as-is; build hosted-hardening paid-tier-ready per the prior; pre-register the Element playbook as the contingency — if a hosted business ever justifies protection, switch future versions to AGPL and add a CLA at that moment only if proprietary exceptions are wanted. The actual moat evidence points to trademark + operational excellence (every fork was constrained by trademarks, cf. Vaultwarden's rename), which argues for keeping D-17's trademark timing tied to the same traction trigger rather than to licensing.

## Open questions

- No court has ruled on unilateral Apache-2.0→AGPL relicensing of a mixed-contributor codebase; before ever executing the switch, get a real legal opinion on notice/NOTICE retention mechanics for third-party DCO contributions (practice says fine, commentary is contested).
- Does an AGPL relay even protect anything for askrelay, given the protocol is open by design and clean-room API reimplementation (Vaultwarden/DocumentDB pattern) defeats code licenses — is the durable moat actually trademark + hosted network effects?
- Quantified AGPL enterprise friction remains anecdotal (Google ban, CNCF policy, VC commentary); no rigorous corporate-policy survey was found — worth a targeted check if an AGPL move is ever seriously staged.
- At what community size does the relicensing goodwill cost become material for a solo-maintainer project — is there a fork-risk threshold (contributors? dependent companies?) that should be written into the pre-registered kill/trigger criteria?
- If a paid hosted tier launches over Apache-2.0 code with no license change, what specifically must the hosted-hardening work package keep proprietary-adjacent (ops tooling, billing, multi-tenancy) versus open, to preserve the ops moat without violating the flagship-OSS framing?

I verified it directly against Apache-2.0 §2 ('perpetual, worldwide, non-exclusive, no-charge, royalty-free, irrevocable copyright license to reproduce, prepare Derivative Works of, publicly display, publicly perform, sublicense, and distribute') and §4 (notice retention) at https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0 — accurate, but it is the single most load-bearing claim and should carry the primary citation.
- ⚠️ 'AGPL-inbound would lock, since AGPL→permissive needs every contributor's consent' — stated as fact with no source. It is the standard reading (GPLv3/AGPLv3 §10 forbids sublicensing and imposing different terms), but the brief cites no license text or authority for this direction of the asymmetry.
- ⚠️ The tldr/assessment claim that clone-and-undercut is 'real but only for hyperscaler-scale targets' is contradicted by the brief's own Plausible evidence: Plausible's stated relicensing trigger was an ordinary corporation that closed-sourced and resold its code while the project was tiny. The correct narrow claim is that FATAL/fork-scale clone threats have only come from hyperscalers; small-scale proprietary cloning happens at any size.
- ⚠️ 'No court has ever ruled on unilateral Apache→AGPL relicensing' — an unverifiable universal negative; consistent with finding no case in my searches, but it cannot be positively confirmed and is properly flagged only in the open questions.

- ⚠️ 'cf. AWS DocumentDB vs MongoDB' as the clean-room-API-reimplementation precedent — uncited; true (DocumentDB, launched Jan 2019, implements the MongoDB 3.6 API without MongoDB's code), but carries argumentative weight without a source.
